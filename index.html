You are wearing a fuel gauge for your sugar.

That is the whole idea. A finger stick tells you one number, one time. This little sensor on the back of your arm keeps telling you, all day and all night, without a single poke.

Everything here comes from Dexcom's own guides: the G7 User Guide, the G7 15 Day User Guide, the Start Here guide, App Essentials, and Using Your Receiver. Every card has a line at the bottom that opens up the manual's exact words, so anything on this page can be checked against the real thing. Nothing here is medical advice. The two rules

When to trust it, and when to get the meter out

Out of this whole manual, these are the two rules that actually keep you safe. Everything else is convenience.

Rule one: no number, or no arrow, means no decision

If the screen is missing either one, do not use it to decide anything. Get the finger stick meter out and use that instead.

This includes the warmup period after you put on a new sensor, and any time a system message like Signal Loss is on the screen. The device is not lying to you, it just does not have enough to go on yet.

The manual's exact words

"No number or no arrow, no CGM treatment decision: Use your BG meter to make treatment decisions when your Dexcom G7 CGM System doesn't show both a number and trend arrow as well as during the 30-minute sensor warmup period."

Dexcom G7 User Guide, ch. 2, Warnings

Rule two: how you feel beats what it says

If you feel low but the screen says you are fine, believe your body. Wash your hands with soap and water, do a finger stick, and treat off the meter.

The manual has a saying for this one, and it is worth remembering word for word: when in doubt, get your BG meter out. Shaky, sweaty, confused, or just not right, and the number does not match. That is exactly the moment the meter earns its keep.

The manual's exact words

"When how you feel doesn't match your sensor reading, use your BG meter to treat even if you have a number and arrow. In other words, when in doubt, get your BG meter out."

"Don't ignore low/high symptoms: Use your BG meter to make treatment decisions when your sensor readings don't match your low/high symptoms. If needed, seek immediate medical attention."

Dexcom G7 User Guide, ch. 4, Treatment Decisions; ch. 2, Warnings

Why the meter and the Dexcom never quite agree

They are measuring two different things. The finger stick reads sugar in your blood. The sensor reads sugar in the fluid between the cells just under your skin, which the manual calls interstitial fluid.

Think of it like reading the temperature in the next room over. It is accurate, but it runs a few minutes behind. When your sugar is moving fast, the gap looks bigger. When it settles, the two numbers come back together. Neither one is broken.

Two other honest reasons for a mismatch: unwashed hands on the finger stick, which is the most common cause of a bad meter reading, and the first day of a new sensor, when the two run further apart and then close up over about 24 hours.

The manual's exact words

"One reason is they're measuring glucose in different fluids. The BG meter measures blood glucose while the sensor measures interstitial fluid."

"Hand cleanliness: Wash your hands with soap and water (not hand sanitizer) and dry them. Then test. Many inaccurate BG meter values are from hands not being washed thoroughly before testing."

"Sensor's first day: With newly inserted sensors, the differences between your BG meter and the sensor reading may be greater. Generally, the numbers get closer over the first 24 hours."

Dexcom G7 User Guide, ch. 11, Accuracy and calibration

The arrow

The number is the speedometer reading. The arrow is the speed.

A 70 sitting perfectly still and a 70 dropping like a stone are two completely different situations. This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that gives you your warning time.

What the arrow is worth

Rising, so a little more insulin than usual might be right. Falling, so a little less. That is the manual's own guidance, and it is a conversation to have with the doctor before you start doing it, not something to work out on your own.

The manual's exact words

"Talk to your healthcare provider about using the trend arrows to determine how much insulin to take. Trending up: Consider taking a little more insulin than usual when your glucose is rising. Trending down: Consider taking a little less insulin than usual when your glucose is falling."

Dexcom G7 User Guide, ch. 4, Using the trend arrows

Which sensor is on his arm?

There are two versions in circulation and they differ in exactly two numbers. Check the sensor box: if it says Dexcom G7 15 Day, tap the second button. If it just says Dexcom G7, leave it as it is.

There are two versions of the sensor. Check the box. If it says Dexcom G7 fifteen day, the sensor lasts fifteen days and warms up in sixty minutes. If it just says Dexcom G7, the sensor lasts ten days and warms up in about thirty minutes. Everything else about the two is the same.

The manual's exact words

"Each sensor session lasts up to 10 days with a 12-hour grace period at the end." Warmup: "Shorter warmup — less than 30 minutes."

"The sensor session lasts up to 15 days followed by an extra 12-hour grace period." Warmup: "during the 60-minute sensor warmup period."

Alarms

Every alarm it can make, one at a time

There are only two families. Glucose alarms are about your sugar. System alarms are about the equipment. Knowing which family you are looking at answers most of the panic before it starts.

Why it will not stop beeping

Because you have not pressed OK yet. An alarm repeats every 5 minutes until it is acknowledged. That is deliberate, not a fault. Deal with the situation first, then press OK.

If the alarms are genuinely too much, that is a fixable settings problem and not something to endure. You can change the sound, set most alarms to vibrate, delay the High alarm so it does not fire while insulin is still working, or turn Snooze off so it stops repeating. There is a whole section of the manual on quieting it down, and it is covered in chapter 5 below.

The manual's exact words

"When you get an alert, your first priority is to resolve it: make a treatment decision or fix a system issue. Afterward, acknowledge the alert... Until you acknowledge the alert, it re-alerts every 5 minutes."

Dexcom G7 User Guide, ch. 5, Responding to alerts

For the family

You can watch his numbers from your own phone

This is the feature most families do not know exists, and for an older parent living independently it is the whole reason to have a G7 rather than a meter.

How it works, in one paragraph

He turns on Share in his Dexcom G7 app and invites you by email. You install a separate free app called Dexcom Follow on your own phone. From then on his number lands on your phone every 5 minutes. You can set your own alarms for it, so your phone can wake you if he goes low at three in the morning.

He stays in control the whole time. He chooses whether you see just the number or the graph too, and he can pause or remove any follower whenever he likes. You cannot change anything on his end. Invitations expire after 7 days, so accept it promptly.

This needs the phone app. It does not work from the receiver on its own.

The one hard rule for followers

Never treat off the Follow app. What you are seeing is always older than what he is seeing.

If his number looks alarming on your phone, the move is to call him and have him look at his own screen. His device is the one that makes decisions. Yours is a smoke detector, not a thermostat.

The manual's exact words

"Use as secondary notice: Your Followers' information is always older than yours. Use your current information to manage your diabetes, not your Followers' information. The information they get isn't meant to be used for treatment decisions, analysis, or teaching."

"Use your Dexcom G7 CGM System to make treatment decisions: Don't use Followers' information for treatment decisions, like treating for a low or dosing for a high."

Dexcom G7 User Guide, ch. 2, Share and Follow safety statements

Setting it up

On his phone: Connections > Share, turn Share on, then Invite Follower. Enter your name and email. He picks what you get to see and taps Send Invitation.

On your phone: open the invitation email on the phone itself, tap the link, install Dexcom Follow, accept. Then set your own notification levels in the settings gear next to his name.

As a follower you can set a Low, a High, and a No Data alert, and each one has a For More Than delay so you are not woken by a two minute dip. Urgent Low is fixed at 55 for followers too.

One catch worth knowing: both apps have to be running, both phones need internet, and both need battery. If his phone is dead, you see nothing. That is not a fault, it is just the nature of it.

The manual's exact words

"Your Followers don't need to have the Dexcom G7 phone or watch apps on their smart devices. They only need to download the Dexcom Follow app."

"Invitation Sent: You invited a Follower. They haven't accepted yet. They have 7 days to accept."

"Both apps (Dexcom G7 and Dexcom Follow) must be open or running in the background. The smart devices must work, be connected to the internet, and have charged batteries."

Dexcom G7 User Guide, ch. 7, Share and Dexcom Follow

When to pick up the phone

Dexcom answers 24 hours a day

Dexcom technical support, free, day or night

1-888-738-3646

In the app you can also reach them at Profile > Contact. A free printed copy of the user guide can be ordered from the same number.

Call them when:

Worth a call
  • Signal Loss has gone on for more than 30 minutes
  • Brief Sensor Issue has gone on for more than 3 hours
  • A sensor failed early, or the patch fell off early. They replace them.
  • The applicator will not come off after inserting
  • The receiver shows a System Check error code. Have the code ready.
  • The speaker test does not beep and vibrate

Call the doctor instead when: the site is red, swollen, sore, or itching badly; a sensor wire has broken off under the skin; or you want to change any alarm level, because those are clinical decisions.

Do not wait for anybody when: he is having symptoms of a severe low. Treat it, and if it is not resolving, get emergency help. The manual says it plainly: if needed, seek immediate medical attention.
